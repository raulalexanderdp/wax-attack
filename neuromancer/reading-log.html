Part One: Chiba City Blues

Nothing below reaches past the end of chapter 1.

What happened

We open in the Chatsubo, a bar in Night City, the lawless strip of Chiba City where Case has washed up. Ratz runs the bar with an antique Russian prosthetic arm and a mouthful of rotting steel, and he's the closest thing Case has to a friend.

The backstory arrives in pieces rather than in a block. Case was a console cowboy — a freelance hacker of the first rank, jacking into the matrix for people who paid well. He stole from an employer. Instead of killing him, they poisoned him with a Russian mycotoxin that burned out the part of his nervous system that let him jack in. He came to Chiba because its black clinics fix things nobody else will touch. They couldn't fix him. So he hustles as a middleman, buys and sells things he shouldn't, takes too many stimulants, and is fairly openly trying to get himself killed.

Then the plot starts moving. Linda Lee — his girlfriend, herself wrecked — warns him that Wage, a dealer he owes, wants him dead. Case goes to Julius Deane, the information broker, who is 135 years old on a diet of gene surgery and serums and runs his business out of an office stacked with cartons of ginger. Deane tells him nobody's put money on his head. Leaving, Case realizes he's being followed.

He tracks Wage down, and the confrontation doesn't go the way his fear had scripted it: Wage isn't the one who wants him dead. Case goes home to his coffin — the rented capsule he sleeps in — and finds the lock broken and his stolen RAM gone, Linda with it. And someone is inside waiting for him: a woman in mirrored glasses, with a fletcher pointed at him.

What to notice

The first sentence. The sky over the port is the color of television tuned to a dead channel. Nature described in terms of a machine, rather than the other way around — that inversion is the whole book's logic, stated in fourteen words before you've met anyone. Worth knowing the line has aged sideways: in 1984 a dead channel meant grey static, and a modern reader pictures a flat blue screen. The image survives either way, which is its own kind of luck.

Gibson refuses to explain. Terms land mid-sentence as though you already live here — matrix, deck, coffin, joeboy. There is no dossier, no character stopping to define things for your benefit. This is the technique the whole genre took from him, and it's worth being patient with: you're meant to acclimate, not to be briefed. If you feel half-lost in chapter 1, you're reading it correctly. Most of it resolves by feel within fifty pages.

Case's wound is the engine. Read carefully what he's actually mourning. It isn't the money or the career — it's that he could once leave his body, and now he can't. The novel's contempt-word for flesh is meat, and Case uses it about himself. That's a junkie's relationship to transcendence, and Gibson is quite clear-eyed that it's an addiction rather than an enlightenment. Every wrecked person in this chapter wants out of something.

The bones are detective fiction. A burned-out man with a debt, an information broker, a woman who brings a warning, a threat that isn't what it looks like, and a job about to walk through the door. Gibson is running Chandler's chassis with new bodywork, and he knows it. The novelty is the setting; the plot machinery is deliberately old and reliable.

Ratz's verdict on Night City — a deranged experiment in social Darwinism run by a bored researcher holding down fast-forward — is the thesis of the setting. Nobody here is building anything. It's a place that exists to consume people at speed, and Case likes it for exactly that reason.

The prose. Dense with nouns, thick with brand names, almost no exposition, sentences that cut rather than transition. It reads fast and leaves residue.

Worth remembering

Not explanations — just things that will matter. No hints about how.

  • Linda Lee: pay attention to the gap between what Case says about her and how the prose treats her.
  • Julius Deane — his age, his manner, and how he handles a question.
  • Someone besides Wage has been taking an interest in Case's debts.
  • The woman in the mirrored glasses.
  • The specific language Case uses for the matrix — the phrasing recurs.
  • The Chatsubo and Ratz, as a fixed point to measure distance from.
